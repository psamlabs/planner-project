# LinkedIn Templates — "Pranjal the strategist"

> Warm but sharp. Insight-forward. Less lifestyle, more professional insight.
> Every post runs Notice → Connect → Reframe and keeps the promise: *one new thought.*
> Formatting: strong first line (the feed only shows ~2 lines before "…more"), short paragraphs,
> generous white space, no walls of text. 3–8 tight paragraphs is the sweet spot.

---

## The 4 LinkedIn formats

### Format A — The Overlap (your signature)
Take a thing from one world and connect it to a work/brand idea. This *is* the moat.

```
[NOTICE] One or two lines: a small, real, concrete observation. Sensory. No preamble.

[TURN] A line that signals "here's where it gets interesting" — the pivot.

[CONNECT] 2–4 short paragraphs bridging the observation to a marketing / behaviour / brand idea.

[REFRAME] The one new thought, stated cleanly. The line people screenshot.

[INVITE] A genuine question that makes them apply it to their own work. Never bait.
```

### Format B — The Work Story
A real moment from 5+ years of work → a lesson about people.

```
[SCENE] Drop into a specific moment. "A founder once told me…" / "We rewrote one subject line 14 times."
[TENSION] What was actually at stake beneath the surface.
[INSIGHT] What it taught you about how people/brands/trust really work.
[REFRAME] Zoom out: the thing most people get wrong about this.
[INVITE] "What's the smallest change that made the biggest difference for you?"
```

### Format C — The Reading-into-Work bridge (Pillar 1 → 5)
A book/essay/Substack → a branding or behaviour idea.

```
[HOOK] "I read a line this week that I can't stop applying to marketing."
[QUOTE/IDEA] The idea (attribute honestly). Keep it short.
[CONNECT] How it reframes something in brand/content/behaviour.
[REFRAME] The transferable thought.
[INVITE] "What are you reading that keeps leaking into your work?"
```

### Format D — The Quiet Contrarian
Reframe a piece of accepted marketing wisdom — curiosity-first, never edgelord.

```
[COMMON BELIEF] State the thing everyone repeats. Fairly, not strawmanned.
[FRICTION] The small real observation that made you doubt it.
[REFRAME] The more useful way to see it.
[NUANCE] One honest caveat (this builds trust; you're thinking, not dunking).
[INVITE] "Where does this break down for you?"
```

---

## Hook rules for LinkedIn
- The first line must earn the second. Assume the reader is skimming a fast feed.
- Lead with the **concrete noticing**, not the abstract claim. ("The café never asks 'for here or to go?' until after…") 
- Avoid: "Unpopular opinion", "Let me tell you a story", "🚨", "Here are 5…".
- One idea per post. If you have two, that's two posts.

## Worked examples (in voice — placeholders in [brackets])

### Example 1 — Format A (Overlap: café → onboarding) · Pillars 4 + 5

> The café near my flat hands you the cup before they ask "for here or to go?"
>
> I noticed it because every other place asks first — while you're still deciding, still a stranger.
>
> This one commits to you first. Cup in hand, warm, yours. *Then* the question.
>
> It's a tiny sequence. It's also a whole trust philosophy.
>
> Most brands ask for the commitment before they've given anything — the email, the demo, the "quick call." We call it a funnel. The customer feels it as a toll booth.
>
> The café flipped the order. Give first. Ask second. By the time they ask anything of you, you already feel looked after.
>
> Your onboarding is a café counter. The question isn't *how do we capture them faster.* It's *what can we hand them before we ask for anything.*
>
> Where in your funnel are you asking before you've given?

*Pillar 4 (café, Notice) + Pillar 5 (onboarding, Connect). Reframe = "your onboarding is a café counter."*

---

### Example 2 — Format B (Work Story) · Pillars 5 + 3

> A founder once asked me to make his brand "sound more premium."
>
> We tried the obvious things. Better words. Serif font. Slower, more "considered" copy. It all sounded expensive and meant nothing.
>
> Then I watched how *he* talked about the product when he forgot I was listening — to a friend, off the clock. Specific. A little obsessive. Proud of one weird detail no competitor cared about.
>
> That was the premium. Not the polish — the obsession.
>
> Premium isn't a tone you apply on top. It's the number of small decisions a customer can *feel* you cared about. You don't write your way to it. You reveal it.
>
> We threw out the serif and just showed the obsession. It worked because it was true.
>
> What's the one detail your brand is quietly obsessed with — and are you hiding it behind polish?

*Pillar 5 (work) + Pillar 3 (how people read "premium"). Reframe = premium is revealed obsession, not applied polish.*

---

### Example 3 — Format C (Reading → Work) · Pillars 1 + 5

> I read a line in [book / Substack essay] this week I keep applying to marketing:
>
> *"[the idea, honestly attributed]."*
>
> The writer meant it about [original context]. But it explains something I could never articulate about why some campaigns stick and most evaporate.
>
> [2–3 lines connecting the idea to how attention / memory / trust actually works in a campaign.]
>
> The best marketing doesn't ask for more attention. It rewards the attention it already has — so giving it feels good, not spent.
>
> What are you reading right now that keeps leaking into your work? I collect these.

*Pillar 1 → 5 bridge. Reframe = reward attention, don't extract it. Note the soft reading-club seed in the CTA.*

---

### Example 4 — Format D (Quiet Contrarian) · Pillars 5 + 3

> "Know your audience" is the most repeated advice in marketing. It's also where I see the most confident, expensive mistakes.
>
> Because most "audience knowledge" is a demographic — 25–34, urban, likes coffee. That's a description of a seat, not a person.
>
> The thing that actually predicts behaviour isn't who they *are.* It's what they're quietly trying to feel, and what they're afraid of looking like.
>
> "Knowing your audience" isn't data about them. It's being able to finish their sentence — the one they'd never say out loud.
>
> To be fair: demographics still help you *find* people. They just don't help you *move* them.
>
> Where has "knowing your audience" quietly turned into "describing your audience" for you?

*Pillar 5 + 3. Reframe = knowing = finishing the unsaid sentence. Note the honest caveat — thinking, not dunking.*

## LinkedIn CTA bank (invitations, not bait)
- "Where does this break down for you?"
- "What's the smallest change that made the biggest difference?"
- "What are you reading that keeps leaking into your work?"
- "Curious how this lands for people who [do X]."
- Or no CTA at all — a clean reframe can stand alone. Silence can be confident.

## Formatting checklist before posting
- [ ] First line works with the rest hidden behind "…more"
- [ ] One idea, not two
- [ ] There's a real, concrete noticing up top
- [ ] There's a clear reframe (the screenshot line)
- [ ] Curiosity-first, not funny/educational-first
- [ ] No hustle language, no engagement bait
- [ ] Reads warm but sharp — the strategist window
