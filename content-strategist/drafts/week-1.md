## MON · LinkedIn · 🧠 Notice-of-the-Week twin · Pillar 3+4
**The strategic version of this week's flagship (matcha → attention).**

**Copy:**

> I've made my matcha the same way for [months]. It might be the least productive thing I do — and I think it's quietly made me better at my work.
>
> Same bowl. Same whisk. Same two minutes where nothing else is allowed to happen. No podcast. No "while I…". Just the whisk.
>
> For years I treated attention like a budget. Spend it well, waste none, squeeze a second use out of every minute. Optimise.
>
> But a budget is something you spend and refill. Attention isn't that. Attention is the thing my whole life is actually made of — every relationship, every idea, every piece of work is just attention, pointed somewhere long enough to matter.
>
> Which means the "unproductive" two minutes aren't a leak in the budget. They're the rare moment I stop renting my attention out and actually own it.
>
> The best work I've done didn't come from managing my time better. It came from protecting my attention harder — being fully in one thing instead of half in five.
>
> We keep trying to spend attention more efficiently. Maybe the move is to stop treating it like money at all.
>
> Where's your un-optimisable two minutes — and what does your best thinking actually need from you?

## WED · LinkedIn · 💼 Contrarian · Pillar 5+3
**"Know your audience" describes the seat, not the person.**

**Copy:**

> "Know your audience" is the most repeated advice in marketing. It's also where I've watched the most confident, expensive mistakes get made.
>
> Because most "audience knowledge" is a description of a *seat.* 25–34. Urban. Disposable income. Likes coffee and travel.
>
> That's not a person. That's a demographic sitting in a chair.
>
> Five years in, the thing that actually predicts how someone behaves isn't who they are on a form. It's what they're quietly trying to feel — and what they're afraid of looking like.
>
> Two people can share every data point and buy for opposite reasons. One buys the skincare to feel in control. The other buys the same bottle so she doesn't look like she's stopped trying. Same "audience." Different universe.
>
> Knowing your audience isn't having data about them. It's being able to finish the sentence they'd never say out loud.
>
> To be fair — demographics still help you *find* people. They just don't help you *move* them. Reach is a map to the door; it was never the reason they open it.
>
> Where has "knowing your audience" quietly become "describing your audience" for you?

**Hashtags:** none, or `#marketing` `#brandstrategy`.
**Visual:** text-only is strong here. Optional: a plain typographic card with the line *"Data describes the seat. Not the person."*

## FRI · LinkedIn · 📚 Reading-bridge · Pillar 1+5
**A line from what you're reading → reward attention, don't extract it.**

**Copy:**

> I read a line this week I keep applying to marketing — even though it was written about something completely unrelated:
>
> *"[the line — attributed honestly to [author / book]]."*
>
> [Author] meant it about [original context]. But it explained something I could never quite articulate about why some campaigns stay with us and most evaporate the second they end.
>
> Most marketing is built to *extract* attention. Capture it, convert it, get the click before it's gone. It treats attention like a resource to mine.
>
> The work that lasts does the opposite. It *rewards* the attention it already has — it makes paying attention feel good, not spent. You leave the ad, the post, the shop feeling like you gained something, not like something was taken.
>
> Extractive marketing wins the moment. Rewarding marketing wins the memory. And memory is the only thing still working for you tomorrow.
>
> What are you reading right now that keeps leaking into your work? I collect these — half my best strategy notes started as book underlines.
