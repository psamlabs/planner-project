## MON · LinkedIn · 🧠 Notice-of-the-Week twin · Pillar 1+3
**The book idea (love follows attention) translated strategically: brand love is accrued, not won.**

**Copy:**

> I finished [book] last night and I'm a little annoyed at it — in the best way.
>
> Its argument, roughly: we don't choose what we love. We choose what we *pay attention to*, and love is what's left standing after enough attention.
>
> I've been trying to poke holes in it for two days. Instead it keeps explaining things about my work.
>
> Because we talk about "brand love" like it's a prize a campaign can win. One big idea, one launch, one moment — and the audience falls for you.
>
> But if love follows attention, brand love can't be *won* at all. It can only be *accrued* — deposit by deposit, every small moment a person chooses to look at you again and isn't sorry they did.
>
> Which reframes the job. The question isn't "how do we make them love us?" It's "how do we keep being worth a second look — and never waste the look we get?"
>
> Campaigns spike attention. Consistency compounds it. Only one of those becomes love.
>
> What's a brand you've come to love slowly — and can you point to the moment it happened? (I bet you can't. That's the point.)

**Visual:** text-only, or the book on your desk.

## WED · LinkedIn · 💼 Stacked build-in-public — the design lesson · Pillar 5+3
**Beat 3 of the arc: what building series-gap tracking taught about how people finish things.**

**Copy:**

> Building one small feature taught me more about human behaviour than some entire campaigns I've worked on.
>
> The feature: series-gap tracking in Stacked, the book app I shared here recently. It looks at your shelf and quietly surfaces the holes — the trilogy missing its middle, the author you've read three of and always "meant to finish."
>
> I built it assuming the problem was motivation. People abandon series, so remind them, nudge them, re-engage them. Standard playbook.
>
> Then I mapped my own shelf, and the assumption fell apart. I hadn't *abandoned* anything. I still cared about every one of those series. What I'd lost wasn't interest.
>
> It was the thread. Which book was next. Where I'd stopped. What was even missing.
>
> People rarely quit things they love. They lose the thread — and then feel vaguely guilty, and then avoid the thing that makes them feel guilty. The gap isn't a motivation problem. It's a *map* problem.
>
> So the feature doesn't nudge. It just shows the thread: here's where you are, here's the gap. Turns out that's enough. Nobody needs to be re-motivated to finish a story they love. They need to be able to see where they left off.
>
> I keep thinking about how much "re-engagement" marketing is built on the motivation assumption. Most lapsed customers didn't stop caring about you. They lost the thread — and your winback email is guilt-tripping someone who just needed a map.
>
> What's something you "abandoned" that you actually still love? Check: did you lose interest — or the thread?

**Hashtags:** none, or `#buildinpublic` `#productthinking`.
**Visual:** ⭐ Stacked screenshot of the series-gap view if possible — the feature *is* the proof.
**Note:** proof-of-work framing throughout; no CTA, product named naturally mid-post, link stays in bio.

## FRI · LinkedIn · 💼 Work Story · Pillar 5+3
**Rewriting one line 14 times — the lesson wasn't the words.**

**Copy:**

> We once rewrote a single [subject line] fourteen times. I counted, because by version nine I was questioning my career.
>
> Fourteen versions of one line. And here's the thing — versions two through thirteen weren't making the line better. They were avoiding a decision.
>
> Because every rewrite was really the same argument in disguise: who is this actually for, and what are we actually willing to claim? We kept polishing the words so we wouldn't have to answer the question.
>
> Version fourteen took four minutes to write. Not because we finally found the magic phrasing — because someone finally made the call. This is for [the specific person]. We're claiming [the specific thing]. The line wrote itself the moment the decision existed.
>
> I've seen this on every team since. When the copy won't land after three drafts, it's almost never a writing problem. It's an unmade decision hiding inside a document, and everyone's too polite to name it.
>
> Wordsmithing is often just stalling with better vocabulary. Editing is deciding.
>
> What's the longest you've watched a team polish something to avoid deciding something?

**Hashtags:** none, or `#copywriting` `#contentstrategy`.
**Visual:** text-only.
**Fill before posting:** the real artifact (subject line / caption / tagline) and true count — keep the honest number even if it's 9 or 20.
